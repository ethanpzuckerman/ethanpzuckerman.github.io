1. Exploratory data analysis

The agent ran pipeline/eda.py, which also writes every number below to docs/eda/eda_numbers.json. First it checked that the roll's own arithmetic holds. On all 163,135 rows:

  • market value = land + buildings
  • assessed value = land + buildings (assessed)
  • taxable value = assessed − homestead − veteran − first responder − other exemptions, never below $0
  • Parcel ID = Tax Bill #
  • the homestead exemption never exceeds $7,500

Figure 1: What's on the roll, by count and by value (docs/eda/fig1_share_parcels_value.png)

Residential parcels are 74% of the roll but 52% of its $75.7 billion market value. Exempt parcels are only 2.2% of parcels but 21% of the value. These are large institutional properties: City Park ($1.39 billion), the LSU and VA medical centers (about $1 billion each), Tulane, the Convention Center, the Superdome and the Port. None of that value is taxed. Commercial parcels are 4% of the count and 13% of the value. 14,841 parcels have a taxable value of $0: exempt property, vacant land with an "other" exemption, and 4,197 homes whose exemptions cover the whole assessed value.

Figure 2: How residential values are distributed (docs/eda/fig2_residential_values.png)

The median home is valued at $233,600, and half of homes fall between $145,900 and $398,550. The 10% at the top are above $611,800. These are the Assessor's mass-appraisal values as of 01/01/2023, not current sale prices.

Figure 3: Homestead exemption by district (docs/eda/fig3_homestead_by_district.png)

Citywide, 54% of residential parcels have a homestead exemption. That ranges from 61% in District 5 to 33% in District 1. A low share can reflect more rentals, second homes and short-term rentals, or owners who live there but haven't applied. That makes it a useful map for homestead outreach.

Figure 4: The 10% rule, and the freeze exception (docs/eda/fig4_assessment_ratio.png)

For homes without a freeze, 97% are assessed at exactly 10% of market value, the legal ratio in La. Const. Art. VII §18. The 18,474 homes with a special assessment level (freeze) are spread below that, with a median of 7.6%, because their assessed value was locked when the owner qualified while market value rose. This one chart shows two rules from the education outline working in the data.

Figure 5: Are cheaper homes over-assessed? (the client's question) (docs/eda/fig5_sales_ratio_by_price.png)

For 8,604 residential sales from 2023–2025, we compare the roll's market value with the sale price. The middle and top of the market sit at 0.92–0.94× the sale price. The cheapest tenth ($15K–$130K) sits at 1.18×, with dispersion (COD 33) more than twice the IAAO target band of 5–15. Taken at face value, owners of the least expensive homes are assessed at more than they sold for, while everyone else is assessed below. That's the regressivity concern the project's client raised.

Caveats before anyone acts on it:

  • None of the roll's sale prices are marked verified.

  • The cheapest tenth likely includes distressed and non-market sales. Including every deed type (sheriff's sales, successions and other non-SALE transfers) raises that tenth to 1.41×.

  • Values are as of 01/01/2023, so later sales include appreciation.

  • With 2021–22 sales, from before the valuation date (and with slightly different filters: SALE, SALW and CASH documents, prices of $25K or more), the cheapest tenth is at 1.05×. The gap is smaller there, but it points the same way.

A related pattern: 19% of these sales (40% of 2023 sales) carry a roll value at almost exactly 92% of the sale price, and 15% at exactly 92% rounded to $100. That suggests values are reset when a home sells ("sales chasing"), which would also make sales-based accuracy measures look better than they are for homes that haven't sold.

Appendix figure A1: a data-quality warning (docs/eda/figA1_year_built.png)

"Year built" spikes at 1994: 42,390 parcels, of which 31,432 are vacant lots and 6,876 commercial. That's a placeholder, not a building boom. Residential years are rounded too: 79% end in 0 or 5. The app hides 1994 for non-residential parcels and calls residential years approximate.

Verifying a finding independently

We rechecked the Figure 4 finding a second way, with Python's built-in csv reader instead of pandas, reading the raw file directly:

  • No freeze: 99,289 of 102,157 homes at exactly 10% (97.19%)
  • Freeze: 963 of 18,474 (5.21%)

Both match the EDA. We also traced one parcel by hand. For 1205 St Charles Av #607, land $12,700 + building $133,000 = $145,700 market value. × 10% = $14,570 assessed, − $7,500 homestead = $7,070 taxable, exactly what the roll records.

To reproduce it in Excel (the file's 163,135 rows fit within Excel's limit): filter Occupancy Type = RESIDENTIAL and Freeze Code = 0, add a column =Total Assessed Value / Total Market Value, and count the rows equal to 0.1. The result should be 99,289 of 102,157.

2. Data dictionary

Appendix A (docs/data-dictionary.md, generated by pipeline/data_dictionary.py) covers all 115 fields. Each one has a meaning, a status (Verified / Inferred / Unknown), its fill rate, and common values (hidden for owner and mailing fields).

What we couldn't figure out:

  • Code meanings: what Freeze Code 1–4 each mean, the "other exemption" codes, the LTC subclass codes, and most of the 434 instrument-type codes.

  • The assessment date: whether 01/01/2023 on every row is the 2024 reappraisal's valuation date.

  • Mislabeled fields: "Living Square Feet" is actually a record count, not square footage.
  • Empty fields: 39 are always blank, zero or "NOT AVAILABLE", including building size, bedrooms, bathrooms, condition, tax-sale status and special taxing district.

The chatbot says "I don't know" for all of these.

3. Address matching

Method (address.js, lookup.js): normalize the owner's input and the roll's addresses the same way. That covers:

  • suffixes: Avenue/AV, Boulevard/BL, Highway/HW, Parkway/PW
  • Saint/St and General/GEN
  • MLK and O.C. Haley spellings
  • the 2021 street renames
  • units (Apt / # / Unit, or a bare unit as the roll writes it)
  • half addresses (1/2 vs HF)
  • city and ZIP tails

The matcher then looks up street + house number, falls back to close spellings, and accepts a 9-digit parcel ID.

Tested on 19 addresses (full table in Appendix B; node tests/address_eval.js): 19 of 19 behaved as expected, with expected results written before running:

  • 13 exact matches, including:
    • "1205 St. Charles Avenue, Apt 607, New Orleans, LA 70130"
    • "2000 MLK Blvd"
    • "1050 Jefferson Davis Parkway" (now Norman C Francis)
    • "1518 1/2 Melpomene St"
    • a parcel ID
  • 1 close match to confirm: "1116 Tchoupitoulis St" (misspelled).

  • 2 multiple matches, as designed:

    • "1205 Saint Charles Ave" has 225 condo units, so the app asks for the unit number.
    • "701 S. Claiborne Ave" is 3 parcels, because the roll drops N/S. The app ranks the one whose legal description says "S CLAIBORNE" first.

  • 3 not found, each with a reason:

    • the house number isn't on that street
    • Metairie is outside Orleans Parish
    • no house number was given

Why matches fail or need a choice:

  • The roll has no N/S direction.
  • 1,110 addresses are shared by more than one parcel (condos and stacked records).
  • 1,004 address strings have no usable street.
  • Vacant lots often have no real street address.

4. Outside data

Source: U.S. Census Bureau American Community Survey, 2024 5-year estimates (2020–2024), for all 184 Orleans census tracts (371,853 residents). It covers:

  • race and ethnicity (B03002)
  • median household income
  • owner vs. renter households
  • median home value and median rent
  • residents 65+
  • vacant units

These are the same tables PolicyMap and Social Explorer show.

How it was pulled: by API with pipeline/fetch_acs.py, through Census Reporter's public API, which needs no key. The Census Bureau's own API now requires a free key; set CENSUS_API_KEY and the same script uses it instead.

Saved to: data/outside/acs_orleans_tracts.csv, with a margin of error for every estimate, and .meta.json (endpoint, release, tables, pull date).

Can it be matched to the roll? Tested (docs/outside-data-matching.md, pipeline/geocode_sample.py). The roll has no coordinates or tract, so we sent a random sample of 504 parcels to the Census batch geocoder and joined the tract it returned to the ACS table.

Share of the sample
Joined to an ACS tract, all parcels 86% (432 of 504)
Homes 94%
Commercial 81%
Vacant land 57%
Ties: two possible locations, mostly N/S streets the roll can't tell apart 6%

Verdict: reliable enough for neighborhood context on homes, but not for every parcel. Vacant lots often have no mailable address. A parcel-ID join to the City's parcel GIS layer is the next thing to test. Tract figures describe a neighborhood, never an owner, so they stay out of property answers for now (CLAUDE.md §3 rule 7).

4b. Comparing years (2026 vs 2027)

The assignment asks for 2025 vs 2026 "where available." The 2025 Orleans roll isn't available: the course Box folder has only 2026, and the Louisiana Tax Commission's site keeps only its two most recent upload years. Those years hold the 2026 roll and the new 2027 roll (filed Sept. 4, 2026), so we compared those, by parcel ID (pipeline/ltc_roll.py, pipeline/compare_rolls.py, full write-up in docs/year-comparison.md).

Parser check first: our parse of the raw 2026 tax commission files reproduces the course's Box file exactly. Market, land and taxable values match on all 163,135 parcels.

  • Parcels: 162,940 are in both rolls. 195 drop out and 198 are new (splits, merges or renumbering), and these are reported, not guessed.

  • Values barely moved: 98% of parcels have exactly the same market value, and every parcel is still dated 01/01/2023. Of the increases, 55% are on parcels with a recorded sale since 2025. The rest are likely construction, demolition or corrections (not checked).

  • Taxes moved because millage went up: the median home's total rate went from 131.99 to 133.48 mills. 84% of homes' computed tax rises, with a median increase of $25. The median homesteaded home goes from $1,965 to $2,032.

  • This contradicts our earlier sources, which said the 2027 roll would carry a parish-wide reappraisal. The filed roll doesn't show one. The chatbot now says what the filed roll shows and that it doesn't know why. Ask the Assessor's Office (verification checklist).

Bonus from the same files: each parcel's millage rows give its actual computed tax, so the chatbot no longer estimates. The unverified 121.20-mill figure was too low; the real 2026 East Bank total is 131.99 mills. The files also confirm that homestead doesn't reduce the police and fire millages "without homestead exemption" (11.51 mills).

Beyond the ten: node tests/property_sweep.js asks all 14 property question types about 305 random parcels of every kind (homestead, rental, commercial, vacant, exempt, frozen, veteran, multi-record), 4,270 answers, and flags broken output or a "matches the recorded amount" claim that isn't true. It found two bugs, both fixed:

  • Doubled mills: on 866 parcels with several assessment records, the displayed rate counted each levy twice. The recorded taxes were always right. After the fix, every one of 150,793 parcels without other exemptions reconciles: assessed × mills − homestead = recorded tax, within $2.

  • Wrong routing: "I think my value is wrong" was answered as a value lookup instead of with the review and appeal path.

The final sweep: 0 problems.

Remaining limitations

  • No 2025 roll anywhere we can reach, so "change from 2025" is answered with 2026 → 2027 and a clear note.
  • The 2027 reappraisal question is open: the filed 2027 roll keeps 98% of values. Confirm with the Assessor.
  • Taxes exclude special-district fees (DDD, security districts). They aren't in the state roll files.

  • Demographics aren't in property answers yet. Matching works for 94% of homes but not for vacant land; a parcel-ID GIS join is next.

  • The chatbot is still keyword-based, with no AI model behind it. Property answers are templates filled from the record, so they can't make up numbers, but they only cover the question types in property.js.

  • Code meanings are unknown (freeze codes, exemption codes), so the bot says it doesn't know them.
